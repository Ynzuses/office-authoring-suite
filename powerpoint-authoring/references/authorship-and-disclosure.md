# Authorship, coursework and AI disclosure

Use this reference when producing an author's own presentation or a required AI-use statement. The deliverable speaks to its audience, not to the person commissioning it.

## Voice and evidence

- For individual work, use first person for owned actions, choices and assessments: “I selected this case”, “My assessment”, “I used AI to help draft and check…”. For a team use we/our when ownership is shared. Follow an explicit institutional or impersonal style if required. Do not mechanically change every subject to “I”: a hospital's reported results still belong to the hospital, and quotations retain their original voice.
- Check AI disclosures, captions, conclusions, notes and appendices as well as the main slides. “The student's workspace” and “the student remains responsible” are inappropriate when referring to the presenter of their own work. An example is “I remain responsible for the final content.” This does not assert that all checks or personal review were completed.
- Keep facts, calculations and interpretation separate. Do not draw a generic framework step into an observed process without labelling it as a benchmark or recommendation. Do not manufacture personal research, interviews, review, or independent verification to make the voice sound natural.
- Keep rubric commentary, editing directions and internal QA status out of substantive slides. A required methodology/AI appendix may explain relevant production methods, but “this meets the high-score rubric” is an authoring note, not an analytical claim.

## What an AI disclosure needs to describe

Distinguish three things: the actual content-development prompts; the persistent context that influenced the work; and the tools, outputs and checks used. A workflow summary is not a verbatim prompt transcript.

When all prompts are required, retain the actual user instructions across drafting, generation and revision, with only private paths or unrelated identifiers redacted and the redaction labelled. Do not invent plausible prompts or silently replace them with a polished workflow summary. Put full records in the allowed appendix/notes/attachment only if the assignment accepts that location; keep the visible slide clear about where the record is. If page rules conflict with a mandatory disclosure, establish the applicable rule before calling the deck compliant.

Summarize relevant persistent instructions without exporting private workspace content: assignment/rubric routing, course memory, source records, reusable authoring skills and file checks. Describe only the skills and operations actually used. A truthful statement may be “I used AI to develop a draft and refine the slide structure”; do not reduce substantial drafting assistance to spelling correction or imply completed student review without evidence.

A public repository link can make a reusable workflow inspectable. Prefer the relevant repository or versioned skill source over an unrelated profile, verify its exact URL and accessibility, and label its role as a workflow resource. A user's statement that it is published does not supply a verifiable URL; attribute that statement if verification is unavailable. Never infer that publication guarantees quality, independent case evidence or a higher mark. Do not publish a local workspace, invent a URL, or turn disclosure into promotion. Updating the local skill does not by itself authorize updating a public repository.

## Feedback after delivery

Generalize a demonstrated defect to comparable slides and future tasks. Preserve the author's accepted reasoning and avoid expanding a targeted correction into unrelated redesign. When a deck has been submitted, record submission as user-reported unless platform evidence was actually seen. Keep the submitted artifact unchanged; any subsequently authorized revision receives a separate identity. Record the local hash without claiming it equals the uploaded file unless that identity is established.
